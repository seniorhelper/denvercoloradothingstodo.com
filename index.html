Quick answer

The best things to do in Denver, Colorado are Red Rocks Park and Amphitheatre, the Denver Museum of Nature & Science, Meow Wolf Convergence Station, Denver Botanic Gardens and Denver Union Station. Red Rocks is free to visit on non-event days and sits about 15 miles west of downtown in Morrison. The rest cluster within a few miles of the city center, so a car is optional inside Denver but useful for the foothills.

Denver sits at exactly 5,280 feet — one mile above sea level. That single fact shapes almost everything about a visit here: the air is thinner and drier, the sun is stronger, the sky is a deeper blue, and your first day should be lighter than you planned. Every attraction below includes what it costs, how long it takes, and whether there is a way to do it for free.

The best things to do in Denver, explained

Ranked by how often a visitor finishes the day glad they went. Prices and hours were confirmed in August 2026, but venues change them, so the official site always wins.

01

Red Rocks Park & Amphitheatre

Morrison · 15 miles west of downtown · Free

Most people think Red Rocks is a concert venue you need a ticket to see. It is actually a free city park with a concert venue inside it. The amphitheatre sits in an 868-acre park owned by the City and County of Denver, and admission and parking cost nothing on non-event days.

Two sandstone monoliths, Ship Rock and Creation Rock, frame a bowl that seats 9,525. Crews from the Civilian Conservation Corps carved the seating by hand starting in 1936 and finished in 1941. The venue books roughly 150 to 200 shows a year, and on show days the bowl closes early — often by 2 p.m. — for sound checks.

What to actually do here in daylight: walk the Trading Post Trail, a 1.4-mile loop with about 250 feet of climbing that starts at the Trading Post building and threads past Cave Rock and the gap between Ship Rock and Creation Rock. Give it 45 minutes to an hour. If you only have half that, take the Geologic Overlook Trail, roughly half a mile round trip from near the top of the Upper South lot.

Then walk into the bowl itself. On a weekday morning you will share it with runners doing stair repeats at 6,450 feet, which is about 1,200 feet higher than downtown Denver. The Visitor Center at the top holds a small museum on the geology and construction plus the Colorado Music Hall of Fame. Thirty minutes is plenty.

Check before you drive out. Park hours run one hour before sunrise to one hour after sunset, but on concert days the amphitheatre and Visitor Center close early and closing times are not set until the week of the show. The daily recorded line is 720-865-2494. Sunrise on a clear morning is the single best free hour in metro Denver.

Free admission Free parking 2–3 hours Elevation 6,450 ft Exit 259 off I-70
02

Denver Museum of Nature & Science

City Park · 2001 Colorado Blvd · Paid, with free days

The single best rainy-day, snowy-day or too-hot-day answer in Denver, and strong enough to be a first choice even in perfect weather. Prehistoric Journey traces life on Earth from 3.5-billion-year-old single-celled organisms to dinosaurs. The wildlife dioramas are among the best in the country. Space Odyssey handles the cosmos, and the Discovery Zone gives small children somewhere to burn energy.

Plan on three hours minimum. Visitors regularly spend five or six. The museum is open daily from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. and stays open until 9 p.m. on many Fridays. Parking is free, which is unusual for a downtown-adjacent attraction.

Money notes. Published adult general admission has been running in the low-to-mid twenties in 2026, with reduced rates for seniors 65 and older and for youth ages 3 to 18. Children two and under are free. The Gates Planetarium, the Sturm Infinity Theater and some temporary exhibitions need a separate timed ticket, including for members. Buy online before you go; walk-up slots for the theaters sell out.

The museum also runs free days and free evenings through the year, and on those dates general admission needs no ticket at all — though the planetarium and temporary exhibits still do. SNAP EBT cardholders can get $3 admission for up to ten people in a party, and school-age Denver residents with a MY Denver Card get in free during defined summer and December windows.

Roughly $23–$26 adult Free parking Free days scheduled 3–5 hours Open daily 9–5
03

Meow Wolf Denver — Convergence Station

1338 1st St · Sun Valley · Timed ticket

Four floors, more than 70 installations, more than 300 artists, and a premise that sounds absurd until you are standing inside it: Convergence Station is presented as Earth's first multiversal transit station, where four alien worlds have collided in one building. You wander. There is no route. Things open when you touch them.

People plan for 90 minutes and stay four hours. That is the normal failure mode here, and it is worth building your day around. There is a bar and food service on the second floor, and a concert venue called the Perplexiplex that books real shows, so the building doubles as a nightlife destination.

Practical notes that save trouble: buy a timed ticket in advance and give yourself at least two hours from booking to entry time. Anyone under 16 needs an adult with them. Large bags and bulky coats have to stay in your vehicle. Flash photography is not allowed. All Meow Wolf exhibitions are Certified Autism Centers, and the Denver location runs sensory-considerate accommodations.

Parking is on site at 1338 1st Street, limited, first come first served, with a daily rate that has been $15. Overflow parking sits at 1408 Zuni Street. On Denver Broncos home game days the whole area gets expensive and congested — Empower Field at Mile High is three tenths of a mile away — so check the schedule before you pick a date.

The building is visible from the I-25 and Colfax interchange but you cannot reach it from the viaduct. Access is from below, through the Auraria Campus or the Stadium District. Follow your map app rather than your eyes.

Timed ticket required 2–4 hours Parking approx. $15 Under 16 with an adult
04

Denver Botanic Gardens

1007 York St · Cheesman Park edge · Paid, with free days

Twenty-four acres and more than fifty distinct gardens, ten minutes east of downtown. What makes this place different from a botanical garden anywhere else is the emphasis on plants that survive a semi-arid mile-high climate. The native collection alone runs to hundreds of species, and eighteen gardens are dedicated specifically to Western flora.

The greatest hits: the Boettcher Memorial Tropical Conservatory, a humid glass house that is a genuine relief in January; the Monet Pool with its waterlilies, best mid-to-late summer; the Japanese garden Shofu-en; and the Mordecai Children's Garden, three acres built on top of the parking structure with hands-on, plant-based play.

Adult general admission has been running around $15 to $17, with reduced rates for seniors, military, students and children 3 to 15, and free entry for children two and under. Tickets are date-and-time reserved, so buy online. The first floor of the Freyer–Newman Center needs no ticket at all, which gets you the Helen Fowler Library and the Copper Door coffee shop for free; the second floor with the art galleries and the free films in the Sturm Family Auditorium is included with admission.

Free days happen several times a year — April 22 and June 9 were on the 2026 calendar — and Kids Free Summer Second Saturdays gave free general admission to children 3 to 15 on June 13, July 11 and August 8, 2026. SNAP EBT holders pay $3 per person for up to six people. The Gardens close on New Year's Day, Thanksgiving and Christmas, and close early for special events, so check the early-closure page before a long drive.

Chatfield Farms in Littleton, at 8500 W Deer Creek Canyon Road, is the Gardens' 700-acre working farm and native-plant site. Very different feel: prairie, a historic homestead, a corn maze in fall and a lavender festival in July. Worth a separate trip, not a same-day add-on.

About $15–$17 adult Free days scheduled 2–3 hours 720-865-3500 Mostly outdoors
05

Denver Union Station

17th & Wynkoop · LoDo · Free to enter

Denver's living room, and the one attraction that costs nothing and still feels like an event. The Great Hall is a restored Beaux-Arts waiting room that anyone can walk into, sit in, and stay in. Locals treat the long leather benches as a co-working space. Visitors treat them as the best free architecture in the city. Both are correct.

The 1914 building went through a $54 million restoration and reopened to the public in July 2014 as a transit hub wrapped around a hotel, restaurants and shops. The historic ticketing office is now Terminal Bar, where you order Colorado craft beer at the window instead of a ticket to the coast. The mezzanine holds the Cooper Lounge, a cocktail bar looking down over the hall. The Crawford Hotel occupies the upper floors. Tattered Cover, a Denver institution since 1971, has a shop off the hall, and Milkbox Ice Creamery and Pigtrain Coffee handle the daytime crowd.

Union Station is also the practical center of a car-free Denver trip. The RTD A Line runs to Denver International Airport in about 37 minutes. The free 16th Street FreeRide shuttle starts here. Buses, light rail and Amtrak's California Zephyr all use the station.

Free programming rotates through the year: live music on the Terminal Bar patio on summer Friday evenings from 5 to 8, urban markets in the plaza, and a holiday market in November and December. On August 1, 2026, the station hosted a Colorado Day celebration marking 150 years of statehood, with free performances in the Great Hall from the Colorado Symphony, Central City Opera and others.

Free to enter 30 min – 2 hours A Line to DIA, ~37 min Restored 2014
06

Denver Zoo Conservation Alliance

City Park · 80 acres · Paid

Founded in 1896 with the donation of an orphaned black bear, the zoo turned 130 in 2026 and ran a summer-long anniversary program. It covers 80 acres inside City Park, holds roughly 3,000 animals across more than 450 species, is accredited by the Association of Zoos and Aquariums, and opens 363 days a year. It is consistently Denver's most-visited paid attraction.

Give it three to four hours if you want to see it properly, and understand that 80 acres at 5,280 feet is more walking than it sounds. Strollers are worth renting. The Conservation Carousel and the Denver Zoo Railroad cost extra and are worth it with small children.

The collection changes, and it is worth checking what is currently on habitat before you build a day around one animal. Stingray Cove is a touch experience. The rhinoceros yard was reworked in recent years, and the zoo's last Nile hippo was transferred to a Texas preserve in spring 2026, ending that part of the collection.

The zoo's signature event is Zoo Lights, which runs on selected nights across December and into early January. It is ticketed separately from daytime admission, sells out, and members get a discount. There is also a bundle that adds hot cocoa and ride vouchers. If you are in Denver in December with kids, put it on the calendar before you book anything else.

The zoo and the Denver Museum of Nature & Science sit on opposite edges of the same park, roughly a mile apart on foot. Doing both in one day is possible but brutal. Doing one plus a City Park picnic and the Mile High Loop is the better version of that day.

Paid admission 3–4 hours Open 363 days Founded 1896
07

Colorado State Capitol & the Mile High Step

200 E Colfax Ave · Capitol Hill · Free

The gold dome is the reason Denver gets to call itself the Mile High City at all. Most of downtown sits just below 5,280 feet. Capitol Hill sits on a slightly higher rise, which pushes the Capitol's west steps over the line. Free guided tours run on weekdays and cover the Senate, the House, the stained glass and the Colorado Rose Onyx wainscoting, a pink marble found nowhere else on Earth. The dome was first covered in gold leaf in 1908 and stands 272 feet.

The famous part is outside and takes two minutes. On the west steps you will find a carved inscription reading "One Mile Above Sea Level." You will also find a brass marker on a different step. Both are correct, in their own era.

University of Denver students first surveyed the mile-high point in 1909 and set a brass marker on the 15th step. It was stolen so many times that in 1947 the state gave up and carved the words straight into the granite. In 1969 Colorado State University engineering students re-surveyed and concluded the mark belonged on a higher step, so a second marker went in. In 2003 better measurements moved it again, and a brass medallion showing the Rocky Mountains was installed at a ceremony with the governor. Three steps, three eras, one mile. Nobody is wrong; the datum kept improving.

Combine the Capitol with Civic Center Park directly west, the Denver Art Museum and History Colorado Center a few blocks south, and the United States Mint one block west. That cluster is the densest free-and-cheap block in the city, and it is all walkable.

Free entry Free guided tours 45–90 minutes Weekdays only Dome is not step-free
08

Denver Art Museum

100 W 14th Ave Pkwy · Golden Triangle · Free under 19

Three buildings on one campus. The Hamilton Building is the jagged titanium wedge designed by Daniel Libeskind — 146,000 square feet of angles that photograph better than almost anything else downtown. The Martin Building holds the deep collections. The Kirkland joined the campus in 2025.

The collections that matter most here are the ones you will not find assembled this way elsewhere: Indigenous Arts of North America, including the Home/Land installation created with Native artists in residence; Latin American art, including the only complete set of Francisco Clapera's casta paintings in an American museum; and a strong Western American holding.

The pricing is the headline. General admission is free every single day for everyone 18 and under, through the museum's Free for Kids program. Adults pay general admission, with peak pricing on Fridays, Saturdays, Sundays and holidays, so a Tuesday visit is cheaper. On top of that the museum runs a free day roughly once a month for everyone. The 2026 schedule included January 13, February 10, March 10, April 26, May 12, June 9, July 14, August 1, September 12, October 13, November 7 and December 8. Register in advance for those.

Hours matter here more than at most museums: the campus is closed on Wednesdays, open 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. Thursday through Monday, and open late on Tuesdays until 8 p.m. Ticketed special exhibitions are separate from general admission, including on free days.

Free for ages 18 and under Monthly free days Closed Wednesdays 2–3 hours Tue open until 8 p.m.

RiNo Art District murals

River North · Larimer & Walnut corridor · Free

An open-air gallery with no ticket, no hours and no fixed route. RiNo — short for River North — is a former industrial district northeast of downtown whose brick walls have been painted, repainted and painted over again for more than a decade. The density is the point: within a few blocks you can stand in front of dozens of large-format works by artists from Denver, elsewhere in the United States and overseas.

Start at Denver Central Market at 2669 Larimer Street. Grab coffee, then walk the parking lot behind it and the alley beside it — that is where the concentration is highest. From there, work north and east along Larimer and Walnut. The two-sided Larimer Boy and Girl mural near 27th and Larimer changes depending on the angle you view it from and is the most photographed wall in the city. The large eye mural wrapping the IMAC building at 2550 Larimer is the other landmark.

Mural festivals held in September bring in muralists from around the world and refresh a large share of the walls each year, which means what you photograph in October will not all be there the following autumn. That impermanence is the honest reason to go now rather than later.

Getting there without a car is easy: the RTD A Line stops at 38th and Blake, two stops from Union Station, and the mural corridor is a ten-minute walk. That matters on First Friday evenings, when the neighborhood fills and street parking disappears.

Free, always open 1–2 hours A Line: 38th & Blake Best light: golden hour
10

United States Mint at Denver

320 W Colfax Ave · Free tour, reservation required

One of only two U.S. Mint facilities open to the public, and the only one that requires a formal guided tour. The Denver Mint has been striking coins since 1906 — the facility traces to an 1863 federal assay operation born out of the Colorado gold rush — and it can produce enormous daily volumes. Every coin struck here carries a small "D."

Booking is the whole game. In-person same-day tickets were permanently discontinued. Reservations are made only through the official U.S. Mint Tour Reservation System, open up to 30 days in advance, released at 1 a.m. Denver time. Tickets are free and limited to five per person per online request. Tours run Monday through Thursday, excluding federal holidays, at 7:15 a.m., 8:30 a.m., 9:45 a.m., 11:30 a.m., 12:45 p.m. and 2 p.m., with a maximum of 50 people per tour and up to six tours a day.

Rules are strict and enforced. Every visitor must be at least seven years old. Arrive at the tour entrance on Cherokee Street thirty minutes before your slot — late arrivals are not admitted. Bring photo ID and your confirmation, printed or on your phone. There is a long prohibited-items list that includes all purses and bags, and the Mint provides no lockers or storage. Photography inside is not allowed. Coin production schedules vary, so there is no guarantee you will see presses running.

The building itself is worth a look even if you never get a ticket. It is modeled on the Palazzo Medici Riccardi in Florence, and the gift shop is open to the public without a tour reservation.

Set an alarm for the 30-day mark. Slots for summer weeks are typically gone within hours of release. If you miss out, the Colorado State Capitol tour one block east is free, needs no reservation, and fills the same slot in your day.

Free tour Reservation only, 30 days out About 45–75 minutes Ages 7 and up Mon–Thu only
11

16th Street

Market St to Broadway · Downtown · Free

Denver's main street, rebuilt from the ground up and reopened in full on October 4, 2025 after three years of construction and roughly $175 million of work. If your last visit was during the construction years, this is a different street.

What changed: transit lanes moved to the center of each block, eliminating the old median and widening the pedestrian walkways to a curbless configuration that can host festivals. More than 950,000 granite pavers were laid by hand, in a pattern that carries forward the original Navajo rug and diamondback rattlesnake design. Over 200 new trees went in, increasing the corridor's tree canopy by about 34 percent, with a new underground soil and irrigation system to keep them alive. Miles of water, sewer and fiber infrastructure underneath were modernized at the same time.

The free 16th Street FreeRide shuttle returned to the full corridor on October 5, 2025. It runs seven days a week, stops at every intersection between Denver Union Station and Civic Center Station, and covers the 1.25 miles end to end in about 15 minutes. During peak periods buses come roughly every traffic-light cycle. It is the single most useful free thing in downtown Denver, and it makes the Union Station–to–Capitol corridor navigable in any weather.

Use 16th Street as a spine, not a destination. Step off it into Larimer Square for historic Victorian storefronts, into Dairy Block for the alley bar scene, and into Writer Square. Ride it end to end once just to see the whole downtown in fifteen minutes for nothing.

Free shuttle 1.25 miles 15 min end to end Reopened Oct 4, 2025
12

City Park & the Mile High Loop

330 acres · East of downtown · Free

Denver's biggest and most useful park, and the setting for both the zoo and the natural history museum. What most visitors miss is the view. Stand on the west shore of Ferril Lake late in the day and you get the downtown skyline in the foreground with the Front Range stacked behind it. It is the best free photograph in Denver and almost nobody who is not local knows to go there.

The Mile High Loop is the other reason to come. It is a marked path that follows the 5,280-foot elevation contour through the park, and it happens to measure exactly five kilometres. Running or walking a route that stays precisely one mile above sea level the entire way is a small, specific pleasure that only exists here.

In summer the park is a working neighborhood park: rec-league kickball, soccer and softball nearly every evening, fishing in the two ponds, paddle boats on the lake. City Park Jazz runs free Sunday-evening concerts from June through August and draws thousands of people with blankets and coolers. It is free, it is genuinely good, and it is one of the most pleasant ways to spend a Denver Sunday.

If City Park is full or far, Denver's other three great free parks are Washington Park — 150 acres, two lakes, a flower garden patterned on Mount Vernon's, a 2.6-mile dirt outer loop and a 2.3-mile paved car-free inner loop; Cheesman Park, a quiet 1.4-mile loop with a pavilion and skyline views, directly behind the Botanic Gardens; and Sloan's Lake, Denver's largest lake, with a 2.5-mile perimeter path and paddleboarding.

Free Mile High Loop = exactly 5K Free jazz, June–August 1–3 hours
13

Confluence Park & the river trails

Cherry Creek meets the South Platte · Free

Denver exists because of this exact spot. Gold seekers camped where Cherry Creek runs into the South Platte River in 1858, and the settlement that grew there became the city. Today it is a small urban park with engineered whitewater chutes, a shallow gravel beach and historical markers explaining the founding.

On a hot afternoon it is the closest thing Denver has to a beach. Expert kayakers run the man-made rapids while families wade in ankle-deep water a few feet away. The shaded area under the bridge runs noticeably cooler than the pavement above it. Bring a towel from May through September and water shoes if you plan to get in — the riverbed is rock, not sand.

The park is also the hinge of Denver's trail network. The Cherry Creek Trail runs southeast from here, paved, for up to about 40 miles, with the first 12 miles reaching Cherry Creek State Park. The South Platte River Trail runs north and south more than 30 miles. Together they form a 70-plus-mile paved backbone that connects downtown, Washington Park, the Cherry Creek shopping district and several breweries without ever putting you in traffic. Rent a bike or a scooter and ride four miles southeast from Confluence to see downtown from the water level.

REI's flagship store sits next door in the restored Denver Tramway Powerhouse, which is worth walking into for the building alone. The Downtown Aquarium, the Children's Museum of Denver at Marsico Campus and Elitch Gardens are all within a short walk.

Free Founding site of Denver, 1858 70+ miles of connected trail 1–2 hours
14

A Denver pro sports game

Coors Field · Ball Arena · Empower Field · Ticketed

Denver supports professional baseball, football, basketball, hockey and soccer, and three of the venues sit close enough to downtown to walk or shuttle to. Which one you catch depends entirely on the calendar, so check dates before you build a trip around it.

Coors Field is the one to prioritise if the season lines up. It opened in 1995 in Lower Downtown, and unlike most modern stadiums it is embedded in the neighborhood rather than surrounded by parking. It also contains the best piece of Denver trivia in any building: one row of seats in the upper deck is purple instead of green, marking the exact 5,280-foot elevation line. You can buy a ticket in the mile-high row.

Ball Arena hosts the Nuggets and the Avalanche and sits just west of downtown near the Auraria Campus, walkable from Union Station along the 16th Street corridor. Empower Field at Mile High hosts the Broncos and is three tenths of a mile from Meow Wolf, which means Broncos home dates make that whole district expensive and crowded — useful to know whether you are going to the game or avoiding it. The Colorado Rapids play at Dick's Sporting Goods Park in Commerce City, which needs a car or transit connection.

Even without tickets, LoDo on a game day is an experience: the blocks around Coors Field between Blake, Market and Larimer fill with patios, and the walk from Union Station to the ballpark takes about ten minutes.

Ticketed Purple row = 5,280 ft Coors Field opened 1995 3–4 hours
15

Elitch Gardens Theme & Water Park

2000 Elitch Circle · Central Platte Valley · Ticketed

A full-size amusement park and water park inside the city limits, which is rarer than it sounds. Elitch Gardens has been operating in some form since the 1890s and moved to its current riverside site near downtown in 1995. Island Kingdom, the water park, is included with admission, so on a hot day it is effectively two parks for one price.

The park runs from spring through fall, with Fright Fest in October and holiday events after that. Daily hours generally start at 10:30 a.m. and end somewhere between 6 p.m. and 9 p.m. depending on the date. Tickets, season passes and parking are sold separately through the official site, and buying online ahead of time is cheaper than the gate.

The signature ride is the wooden coaster, refurbished and relaunched as Twister III: Storm Chaser in June 2025. Beyond that the lineup is a solid regional mix of steel coasters, flat rides and a kids' area, plus a tower with a genuinely good view of the Front Range.

Is Elitch Gardens closing? Not for the 2026 season. The land is the site of The River Mile, a 62-acre mixed-use redevelopment approved by Denver City Council in 2018 that would eventually replace the park with housing, offices, retail and riverfront open space. As of 2026, no closure date has been announced and the developer had not filed site development plans or building permits for the parcels. The park keeps running. If seeing it matters to you, do not wait indefinitely.

Ticketed, buy online Water park included Half to full day Spring through fall

Free things to do in Denver

Denver rewards a low budget better than most American cities. Between city parks, free museum days and a free downtown shuttle, you can fill three days without buying a ticket.

Red Rocks at sunrise

Free park, free parking, open one hour before sunrise. Walk the bowl before the runners arrive.

The Mile High Step

West steps of the Colorado State Capitol. Two minutes, zero dollars, and a story worth retelling.

Free Capitol tour

Guided, weekdays, no reservation. Senate chamber, stained glass, Colorado Rose Onyx.

United States Mint tour

Free, but reserve online exactly 30 days out. Monday through Thursday only.

RiNo murals

Dozens of walls, no ticket, no closing time. Start at Denver Central Market.

The FreeRide shuttle

16th Street, Union Station to Civic Center, every intersection, seven days a week, $0.

Union Station Great Hall

Sit in the best room in Denver for as long as you like without buying anything.

Museums on free days

The Denver Art Museum runs a free day about monthly and is always free for ages 18 and under. Nature & Science and the Botanic Gardens schedule their own.

City Park Jazz

Free Sunday-evening concerts, June through August. Bring a blanket and get there early.

Confluence Park

Wade in the shallows, watch kayakers run the chutes, read the markers about the 1858 camp.

Cherry Creek Trail

Paved, flat, car-free, and it connects most of the city. Walk any four-mile section of it.

Rocky Mountain Arsenal

A national wildlife refuge about twenty minutes from downtown with free admission and a resident bison herd.

Denver's major cultural institutions are funded in part by the Scientific and Cultural Facilities District, a regional sales tax. That funding is the reason free days exist at all. Free-day tickets often have to be reserved in advance and are released on a rolling schedule, sometimes three months at a time on the first of each month. Check the venue's calendar rather than assuming walk-up availability.

Where to point yourself

Denver neighborhood by neighborhood

Denver is a collection of distinct districts inside a fairly small core. Pick two or three per day rather than trying to cross the city repeatedly.

LoDo (Lower Downtown)

The historic warehouse district around Union Station and Coors Field. Red brick, Victorian storefronts, the highest concentration of patios in the city. Larimer Square is the oldest commercial block in Denver and the prettiest street to walk after dark. Dairy Block's alley is a compact, well-executed food-and-drink corridor. This is the default place to stay if you want to walk everywhere.

RiNo (River North)

Murals, breweries, converted warehouses and Denver Central Market. Grittier and younger than LoDo, and the best neighborhood in the city for wandering with a camera. First Friday evenings bring galleries and crowds. Reachable in two A Line stops from Union Station, which is the smart way to visit.

Five Points

Historically the center of Black Denver and once known as the Harlem of the West for its jazz clubs. The Welton Street corridor carries that history on its walls in murals, and the neighborhood's music heritage is genuine rather than marketed. Directly adjacent to RiNo and often walked together.

LoHi & the Highlands

Across the river northwest of downtown, connected by a pedestrian bridge. Great sightline back at the skyline, dense restaurant cluster around 32nd and Lowell and around 16th and Central, and quieter residential streets between. Good for dinner, better for a long walk before it.

Golden Triangle

The museum district: Denver Art Museum, History Colorado Center, Clyfford Still Museum, Kirkland Museum, all within a few blocks of the Capitol and the Mint. If you have one culture-heavy day, spend it entirely inside this triangle and never move your car.

Capitol Hill & Colfax

Dense, historic, walkable, and home to the mansions of Denver's silver-boom era alongside Colfax Avenue's music venues and dive bars. Cheesman Park sits in the middle of it. This is where Denver feels most like a city rather than a downtown.

Cherry Creek

Upscale shopping and gallery district about three miles southeast of downtown, reachable by the Cherry Creek Trail on foot or by bike from the core. Cherry Creek North's tree-lined blocks hold some of Denver's best restaurants. Different pace, no grit.

Baker & South Broadway

Vintage shops, record stores, tattoo studios and independent restaurants along a long commercial strip locally called SoBo. Adjacent Santa Fe Drive is Denver's Art District on Santa Fe, with a long-standing Chicano mural tradition and monthly art walks.

Washington Park & South Pearl

Residential and green, built around the 150-acre park. South Pearl Street runs a farmers market on summer Sundays and has a short, walkable restaurant row. This is where you go to see what living in Denver looks like.

Sun Valley & the Stadium District

Home to Meow Wolf, Empower Field at Mile High and Elitch Gardens, wedged between I-25, the river and the Auraria Campus. Not a strolling neighborhood — a destination district. Check the Broncos schedule before planning anything here.

The thing nobody warns you about

Altitude, sun and weather

Denver's elevation is not a novelty. It changes how you should plan your first 48 hours, how much water you drink and how hard the sun hits you.

Thinner air

Air at 5,280 feet carries roughly 17 percent oxygen against about 21 percent at sea level. You breathe faster without noticing, which costs you fluid.

Drier air

Sweat evaporates before you feel it. Plan to drink noticeably more water than you would at home, starting before you land.

Alcohol hits harder

A dehydrated body at altitude handles drinks badly. Go light the first night. The same caution applies to legal cannabis edibles, which are stronger than most visitors expect.

Symptoms show up late

Most mild altitude symptoms start 6 to 24 hours after arrival: headache, fatigue, light-headedness, poor sleep, low appetite.

Ease into day one

Skip the hard hike, the summit push and the heavy workout for the first 24 to 48 hours. Sightseeing on flat ground is the right first day.

The mountains are higher

Serious altitude illness usually begins around 8,000 feet, and many Colorado mountain towns sit at or above that. Breckenridge is roughly 9,600 feet. Sleep in Denver first if you can.

Mild symptoms usually improve within a few days with rest, water and regular light meals. Symptoms that get worse while you stay at the same elevation are a warning sign, not something to push through. If a headache intensifies alongside nausea, confusion, or breathlessness at rest, descend and get medical care. This page is a travel guide, not medical advice — if you have a heart or lung condition, prior altitude illness, or you are pregnant, ask your own clinician before a high-altitude trip.

Ultraviolet exposure climbs with elevation and Denver has less atmosphere overhead to filter it. Sunscreen is a year-round item here, including on cold, clear January days on the ski slopes and on overcast afternoons. The upside is the reason the sky photographs the way it does: with less water vapour in the air, the blue really is deeper.

You will see the claim everywhere. It traces back to a railroad publicist in the 1870s who was selling migration to Colorado. Measured against National Weather Service sky-cover definitions, Denver averages about 115 clear days, 130 partly cloudy days and 120 cloudy days a year — roughly 245 days with meaningful sun. NOAA's percent-possible-sunshine data ranks Denver near 69 percent of days, about 252 a year. The 300 figure only holds if you count any day the sun appears for at least one hour. Denver is genuinely, unusually sunny. It is just not 300-days sunny.

Mark the calendar

Denver events worth planning around in 2026

Dates confirmed where organisers have published them. Always re-check the official site before booking travel.

Colorado's 150th year

Colorado became a state on August 1, 1876, which made August 1, 2026 the sesquicentennial. Denver Union Station marked it with free performances in the Great Hall from the Colorado Symphony, Central City Opera, Cleo Parker Robinson Youth Ensemble and Mariachi Internacional, plus historic LoDo walking tours. Statehood programming continues at museums through the year.

Great American Beer Festival — October 10–11, 2026

Running since 1982 and the largest ticketed beer festival in the country. For the first time in its history it moves outdoors, from the Colorado Convention Center to Denver's Levitt Pavilion. Hundreds of breweries pour, and the competition awards medals across more than 100 beer and cider styles. Tickets went on sale in June 2026 and sell out.

Denver Wiesn Oktoberfest — October 2026

Scheduled across two weekends, October 16–18 and October 22–25, 2026. Bavarian format, hosted by the Bavarian Guild, at high altitude, which is its own warning.

City Park Jazz — June to August

Free Sunday-evening concerts on the lawn near Ferril Lake. Thousands of people, blankets, coolers, no ticket. Arrive an hour early if you want a decent patch of grass.

National Western Stock Show — January

Two weeks of rodeo, livestock shows, a downtown cattle drive and a trade floor, at the National Western Complex north of downtown. It is the closest thing Denver has to a civic ritual, and it fills hotel rooms.

Denver Arts Week — November

Includes a free Night at the Museums evening — the Denver Museum of Nature & Science ran a free 5 to 10 p.m. window on November 7, 2026. Galleries across the city extend hours.

Zoo Lights — December into January

Selected nights only, ticketed separately from daytime admission, with a members' discount and an adults-only night. Sells out. Book before you book anything else in December.

First Friday art walks — monthly

Galleries and studios open late on the first Friday of each month, most visibly on Santa Fe Drive and in RiNo. Free, crowded, and the easiest way to see Denver's working art scene.

The Red Rocks concert calendar generally runs March through November and books somewhere between 150 and 200 events. If a show is on your list, buy early and plan for the parking reality: lots open two hours before doors, and the walk from the lower lots to the top of the bowl is a real climb at 6,450 feet. Bring a layer — the temperature drops hard after sunset.

Fuel

Where to eat and drink in Denver

Denver's food scene is decentralised. Rather than naming a hundred restaurants that change hands, here is how to find the good ones by district.

Food halls, when you cannot decide

Denver Central Market at 2669 Larimer in RiNo is the best of them: a butcher, a fishmonger, a pasta counter, a coffee roaster and a bar under one roof, in a converted warehouse. Union Station's Great Hall works the same way at a smaller scale, with coffee, ice cream, a deli and Terminal Bar all in one room. Both are good answers for a group that wants different things.

Green chile is the local dish

Denver-style green chile is a pork-and-roasted-green-chile stew, poured over burritos, eggs, fries and almost anything else. It is thicker and more pork-forward than New Mexican versions. Order it smothered. Late September is roasting season, and the smell of chiles in propane tumblers on street corners is one of the genuine markers of a Denver autumn.

Beer, obviously

Colorado has one of the deepest craft brewing scenes in the country and Denver hosts the Great American Beer Festival every autumn. RiNo has the highest taproom density; Terminal Bar at Union Station carries an unusually broad Colorado draft list. Remember the altitude before you set a pace.

Patio and alley clusters

Larimer Square, the block of Larimer between 14th and 15th, is the oldest commercial block in Denver and the most photogenic place to eat outdoors. Dairy Block's Alley behind the Maven Hotel packs several concepts into one walkable corridor. Both are steps off the 16th Street FreeRide route.

Neighborhood restaurant rows

For dinner away from downtown, the reliable strips are South Pearl Street near Washington Park, 32nd and Lowell in the Highlands, Tennyson Street in Berkeley, and Cherry Creek North. Each is a walkable few blocks rather than a single restaurant, so you can decide on arrival.

Markets and coffee

The South Pearl Street farmers market runs Sunday mornings in summer. Union Station's plaza hosts urban markets seasonally, and a holiday market in November and December. For coffee, the first floor of the Freyer–Newman Center at the Botanic Gardens has a shop you can visit without paying garden admission.

Straight answers

Denver things to do — FAQ

What is the number one thing to do in Denver, Colorado?

Red Rocks Park and Amphitheatre. It sits about 15 miles west of downtown in Morrison. Admission and parking are free on non-event days, and the park is open from one hour before sunrise to one hour after sunset. You can walk into the seating bowl, hike the 1.4-mile Trading Post Trail and visit the Visitor Center without buying a concert ticket.

Are there free things to do in Denver?

Yes. Red Rocks Park, the Colorado State Capitol and its free guided tours, the United States Mint tour, RiNo Art District murals, Confluence Park, City Park, Washington Park, Cheesman Park, 16th Street and the RTD 16th Street FreeRide shuttle all cost nothing. The Denver Art Museum is free every day for everyone 18 and under, and major museums run SCFD free days through the year.

How many days do you need in Denver?

Two full days covers downtown and one major museum. Three days lets you add Red Rocks, a neighborhood like RiNo or Highlands, and a foothills drive. Four or more days makes a mountain day trip to Golden, Idaho Springs or Rocky Mountain National Park comfortable rather than rushed. Our Denver itinerary planner lays out each of those days hour by hour.

Is Denver really a mile high?

Yes. Denver's official elevation is 5,280 feet, exactly one mile above sea level. The mile-high point is marked on the west steps of the Colorado State Capitol. Three different steps have carried the marker since 1909 because survey methods kept improving, which is why the carved 15th step and the later brass markers do not agree.

Will I get altitude sickness in Denver?

Most visitors adjust within a day or two, but mild symptoms are common. Air at 5,280 feet holds roughly 17 percent oxygen compared with about 21 percent at sea level. Drink water before and after you land, go easy on alcohol the first night, eat regularly, and save hard hikes or high mountain passes for day two or three. Symptoms that get worse at the same elevation are a signal to slow down and see a clinician.

Does Denver really get 300 days of sunshine?

Not by the strict definition. The slogan dates to 1870s railroad marketing. Using National Weather Service sky-cover categories, Denver averages roughly 115 clear days and 130 partly cloudy days, about 245 days with meaningful sun. NOAA percent-possible-sunshine data puts Denver near 69 percent of days, about 252. The 300 figure only works if you count any day the sun appears for at least an hour.

What is the best month to visit Denver?

September and early October are the strongest all-around window: warm days, cool nights, thin crowds, aspen color in the foothills and the Great American Beer Festival on October 10 and 11, 2026. May and June are close behind for green parks and long daylight. January and February are cheapest, and the National Western Stock Show fills January.

How do you get around Denver without a car?

The RTD A Line train runs from Denver International Airport to Union Station in about 37 minutes. From Union Station the free 16th Street FreeRide shuttle runs the 1.25-mile corridor to Civic Center Station, stopping at every intersection with an end-to-end time near 15 minutes. The A Line also stops at 38th and Blake, a 10-minute walk into RiNo's mural corridor. Red Rocks, Golden and the mountains need a car or a tour.

What is there to do in Denver with kids?

The Denver Museum of Nature & Science, Denver Zoo Conservation Alliance, the Mordecai Children's Garden at Denver Botanic Gardens, Elitch Gardens Theme & Water Park, the Downtown Aquarium, Confluence Park's shallow wading area and the free FreeRide shuttle all work well with children. The Denver Art Museum is free for everyone 18 and under, every day.

Is Denver walkable for visitors?

The downtown core is. Union Station, 16th Street, Larimer Square, the Colorado State Capitol, the Denver Art Museum and the United States Mint sit within roughly a mile of each other. 16th Street reopened on October 4, 2025 after a three-year, $175 million rebuild with wider sidewalks and more than 200 new trees. Outside that core, Denver spreads out and you will want transit, a bike or a car.
